Sivarathri (Maha Shivaratri)

👤 Sathavva🎂 Age 50💼 Agricultural Labourer📍 Jagtial district, Telangana

Speaker Metadata

Sathavva, age 50 · Agricultural Labourer — Jagtial district, Telangana.

Original Telangana Narrative

ఇగ శివరాత్రి పండుగత్తే, ఇగ ఎమన్న రాయేసుని కొంటెతనం, ఎమన్నా కోరుకున్న పనికావలె అది ఇది అంటే నిరారం ఒక్కపోద్దుండి పొద్దుగూకినవలె గింతంత ఏదన్న సత్తసొంటిది చేసుకొని ఇంత ఆడవేట్టుకొని ఇగ ఇంత పువ్వులో, పత్తిరో ఎదో ఎదో జమజేస్కొని ఇగ జాగారం ముట్టిచ్చుకొని పెద్ద రాతిరి దాక అట్ట్నే ఉండి ఇంతంత కొబ్బరికాయ కొట్టి, లేపోతే కొబ్బరికాయకు పైసల్ లేకపోతే ఇంత పప్పున్ను నువ్వులు కలిపి, ఇంతంత శెక్కరి కలిపి, అవ్విలేకపోతే బెల్లమున్ను నువ్వులు కలిపి రాయేసుని పలారముల్లో అని బాజార్ల పంచిపెడుదురు, బజార్ల పంచి పెడ్తే ఆడికి పోరగాండ్లు నాదివేట్టుమని ఈశెయ్యి వాడుదురు మల్ల మావ్వదివెట్టు మని ఈశేయ్యికింద ఈశేయ్యివడుదురు అవ్వి ఇవ్వి ఒక్కండ్లవోసి మల్ల మీదశెయ్యివెట్టి మాతాతదివేట్టువనందురు. గట్ల. నేనైతే కొబ్బరిశిప్పవట్టి చిప్పనింపుకొని అద్దును. మావ్వ పొద్దుగాల తానంజేషి అవ్వివ్వి పనులుసేసినంక, మల్లపోద్దూకి తానంజేసి మా ముసలోనిది పాతదోతి కట్టుకొని జాగారం ముట్టిచ్చి ఒక్కపోద్దిడిషి అదే దోతి మీద కిందవండు. ఇగ సామునముఒక్కపొద్దు, దానికి ఎనుకట దానికి కాలుజేతులు వడిపొయినయట సామునముల ఒక్కపోద్దువట్టి బేడెంచుకుందట పతీసామునముల తానంజేసి ఒక్కపోద్దుండి పొద్దుగూకి ఐదిండ్లు, గిదే! నల్లదదే దోతి మడికట్టుకొని పాతదోతి సంకజోలె కట్టుకోని ఐదిండ్లు రాయేసుని బుచ్చం అని జోలేత్తుకొని ఆ బియ్యం దేవుడచ్చేటోల్లకి ఇచ్చచ్చు. ఎనుకట జాగారం అంటే బాగా బక్తి ఉండే ఇప్పుడు సేపులు తినుడు అంగుర్లు తినుడు అవ్వితినుడు ఇవ్వితినుడు ఎదో ఉన్నం అంటే ఉండుడన్నట్టు ఒక్కపొద్దు ఉండుడు గని ఏమిలేదు.

English Translation

When the Sivarathri festival arrives, if we desire any playful blessing from Lord Shiva (Raayesu), or if we want a specific wish fulfilled, we observe a strict waterless fast (Niraaram Okkapoddu). Once night falls (Poddugookinavale), we prepare a small meal or some sago-like gruel (Satthasontidhi), place it before the deity, gather some flowers and leaves (Patthiro), and light the sacred vigil lamp (Jaagaaram mutthicchukoni). We stay awake until late into the night (Pedda Raathiri). We then crack a small piece of coconut. If we don’t have money for a coconut, we mix roasted lentils and sesame seeds with a little sugar; or if that is unavailable, we mix jaggery and sesame seeds. We distribute this in the streets, calling out, "Here is Lord Shiva’s festive offering (Raayesuni Palaaramullo)!" When we distribute it in the streets, the local children gather, holding out one hand, begging, "Put it in mine!" Then, they stretch another hand beneath the first, shouting, "Put it in my mother’s share!" They gather these portions together, place another hand on top, and plead, "Put some for my grandfather too." That’s how it was. As for me, I would hold a half-coconut shell, fill it up, and serve them. My grandmother (Maavva) would bathe in the morning, finish her chores, and bathe again at sunset. She would then wear my grandfather's (Maamusoloni) old dhoti, light the vigil lamp, break her fast, and sleep on the bare floor wrapped in that very same dhoti. Then there is the Somavaaram (Monday) fast; they say in the past, her limbs had grown weak, but by holding the Monday fast, her health improved. Every Monday, she would bathe, fast until sunset, and then, wearing that same ritually pure (Madi) dark dhoti and carrying an old dhoti tied into a shoulder-bag (Sanka Jole), she would visit five houses. Holding up the bag, she would beg for alms, saying, "Alms in the name of Lord Shiva (Raayesuni Buccham)!" and would later give that collected rice to the wandering ascetics who came to the village. In the old days, the night vigil meant intense, deep devotion. Now, it’s just about eating potato chips, grapes, and munching on this and that. It is as if we are just existing for the sake of it; there is fasting, but the true spirit is gone.

Festival Practices

Observing a waterless fast (Niraaram Okkapoddu); preparing Satthasontidhi (sago porridge/frugal offering); gathering wild flowers and leaves (Patthiro); lighting the ritual night-vigil lamp (Jaagaaram mutthicchu); performing animal/coconuts offerings or cost-effective alternative offerings (sesame seeds mixed with lentils, jaggery, or sugar); communal distribution of Palaaram (offerings) to neighborhood children; complex intersectional rituals where women wear their deceased/elderly husband's old dhotis as a ritual purity garment (Madi); sleeping on the bare floor; observing lifelong Somavaaram (Monday) vows for physical healing; algorithmic begging of raw rice (Buccham) from exactly five households (Aidhindlu) using a cloth shoulder-bag (Sanka Jole); and dedicating the collected grains to visiting religious ascetics (Devudacchetoallu).

Festival Vocabulary

TermMeaning
Raayesdu (రాయేసుడు)A distinct, deeply localized subaltern phonetic corruption of Rajeshwarudu (Lord Shiva of Vemulawada) or Rayalingadu, reflecting an intimate, non-Sanskritized kinship with the deity.
Niraaram (నిరారం)Regional dialectal contraction of Niraahaaram (completely empty-stomach / waterless fast).
Satthasontidhi (సత్తసొంటిది)Refers to Satthu or sago-based, low-cost gruel specifically consumed to sustain energy during religious fasts.
Patthiro (పత్తిరో)Localized form of Patram (sacred leaves/Bilva leaves used for Shiva worship).
Jaagaaram mutthicchu (జాగారం ముట్టిచ్చు)The phrase changes the abstract noun 'vigil' (Jagaranam) into a concrete action of "igniting the vigil light," indicating the physical lighting of the ritual lamp that anchors the night-long awake state.
Buccham / Bucchaalu (బుచ్చం)Dialectal variant of Bhiksham (ritual alms/begging for sacred fulfillment).
Madi (మడి)Ritual purity enforced through strict codes of washing and isolating garments used exclusively for sacred tasks.

Linguistic & Ethnographic Commentary

Cultural Interpretation

Sathavva’s narrative provides raw, unmediated text on how subaltern women navigate poverty, resource scarcity, and bodily health through personalized religious vows. The substitution of a coconut with a mixture of sesame seeds, lentils, and jaggery (pappunnu nuvvulu) reveals a highly adaptive ritual economy. When financial capital is absent, folklore allows the peasant to substitute expensive commodities with everyday kitchen staples without losing ritual validity.

Variation & Change

The speaker sets up a sharp contrast between the past (Enukata) and the present, critiquing the contemporary commercialization of the sacred vigil. The transition from intense bodily devotion and sleeping on bare floors to the casual consumption of modern snack foods ("chips and grapes") is framed as a loss of authentic spiritual substance. The ritual has transformed from an active, body-disciplining ascetic trial into a passive, consumerist leisure event.

Research Note

This text is structurally rich for research in gendered anthropology and subaltern religious studies. It details a specific healing narrative where a woman remedies physical paralysis (kaalujaethulu vadipoyinayi) through the Somavaaram vow, converting her recovered mobility into structured, ritualized begging (Sanka Jole) across five specific houses to feed wandering ascetics. It captures how vulnerable bodies use folk-religious vows to build social agency, physical resilience, and networks of localized charity.
