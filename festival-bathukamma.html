Bathukamma / Boddemma

👤 Swaroopa🎂 Age 50💼 Agricultural Labourer📍 Jagtial district, Telangana

Speaker Metadata

Swaroopa, age 50 · Agricultural Labourer — Jagtial district, Telangana.

Original Telangana Narrative

నేలరోజులాడుదురు ఎనుకట, పెత్రమాస రాకముందు ఒకపున్నమత్తది అప్పుడెంజేత్తురంటే దొరగారాడకు బొడ్డమ్మపున్నమని పోలిసుపటేల్ పెళ్ళామో ఎవలన్న దొరగారి పెళ్ళామో, పోలీసు పటేల్ అంటే ఊరికోగలు ఉంటుండే, ఊల్లెకేలవలచ్చిన ఆపెద్దమనిషిని కలుద్దురన్నట్టు, ఆయినెమాటమీదికేల్లె నడుత్తది ఊల్లె. ఆ పోలిసుపటేల్ కింద వాడకొక్కపెద్దమనిషి ఉంటుండే. ఊల్లె ఎం చేద్దామన్న అందరు పెద్దమనుషులు దొరలూ పెటేల్లు అనుకోని సపాయోనికిచేప్పి దప్పుదాటిద్దురు. ఇగ పటేలు పెళ్లాము ఇంత బొడ్డమ్మను పూదిచ్చి, ఐదంత్రాలో ఎన్నో అంత్రాలు రోజు పుదిచ్చుకుంట ఆపీటను తెచ్చుకుంట బదార్లవెట్టి రోజాడుదురన్నట్టు. అప్పటిసుంది దస్సరా రెపొఎల్ల్లుండోఅనె దాకడుదురు. నెల రోజులడుతుండే ఎనుకట. ఎనుకట నైజాము రాజు మనుషులు మనుల్లళ్ళ ఎవలన్న ఆడోల్లు మంచిగా కనవద్తే పట్టుకుపోయి కరాబుచేత్తురట, ఐతే ఒకనాడు వాల్లకన్ను ఒకఊళ్ళ ఒకపిల్ల మీదవడ్డదట , ఆయింత ఆ ఊల్లె ఆడోల్లు ఆపిల్లను దేవునిగుడ్డ్లె దాసిర్రట, మల్లపోద్దుగాల పొయ్యి సుసేసరికి అక్కడ ఉన్నామె మాయమయ్యి పువ్వులు ఉన్నయట అందరప్పుడు బతుకమ్మ బతుకమ్మ అని మొక్కిర్రట. ఇగ అప్పటిసంది బతుకమ్మ ఆడుడు సురువు అయిందట. ఇప్పటోలె తొమ్మిదిరోజులు గాదు నేలరోజులడుతుండే ఎనుకట.

English Translation

In the old days (Enukata), they would play for an entire month. Before the dark fortnight of Petramaasa (Mahalaya Amavasya) arrives, a full moon night occurs (Boddemma Punnama). At that time, women would gather at the landlord's (Dora) house. The wife of the police-patel or the landlord—since there used to be one police-patel for the entire village—was the supreme authority. Whatever that elder man commanded, the entire village had to follow. Under that police-patel, there was a designated elder for each low-caste hamlet (Waada). Whatever action needed to be taken in the village, all these elites, landlords, and patels would discuss it first, and then order the village servant (Sapaayi) to beat the drum (Dappu) to announce it. Then, the patel's wife would ritually consecrate the Boddemma (earthen mound of the goddess), performing worship for five levels or layers (Anthraalu). Every day, they would re-consecrate it, bring the wooden platform (Peeta) out to the public streets, and play the festive dance. From that day on, they would play until two days before Dasara. In the past, it lasted for a whole month. Back then, during the Nizam King's rule, if his agents saw any woman who looked beautiful, they would abduct her and ruin her modesty (Karaabu chaetthurata). One day, their eyes fell upon a young girl in a certain village. The local village women hid that girl inside the temple shrine to protect her. When they returned the next morning to check, the girl had vanished, and in her place, there was a heap of flowers. Everyone then fell to their knees, praying, "Bathukamma! Bathukamma (Live, Mother! Live!)" From that exact moment, the practice of playing Bathukamma began. It was not just for nine days like it is today; in the past, they would play for an entire month.

Festival Practices

Celebrating Boddemma and Bathukamma for a prolonged full month cycle starting from the Boddemma Punnama (full moon prior to Pitru Amavasya) up until the eve of Dasara; centering festive authority within the Dora (feudal landlord) and Police-Patel estates; utilizing a hierarchical signaling chain from village elites down to hamlet elders (Waada Pedda Manishi); public criers (Sapaayi) broadcasting festive decrees via localized drum beating (Dappu dhaa-thid-dhu-ru); constructing multi-tiered/layered ritual structures (Anthraalu); moving the sacred wooden platform (Peeta) into communal streets for circle dancing; and oral recital of survival myths regarding subaltern female body security under oppressive regimes.

Festival Vocabulary

TermMeaning
Petramaasa (పెత్రమాస)Local Telangana phonetic corruption of Pitru Amavasya, marking the starting point of the transition from Boddemma (played by unmarried girls) to Bathukamma (played by married women).
Anthraalu (అంత్రాలు)Dialectal variant of Anthasthulu (steps/layers), referring to the precise concentric circular tiers constructed when mounting flowers or clay mounds.
Waada (వాడ)The sub-settlement or caste-segregated residential hamlet located on the margins of the main village core.
Sapaayi (సపాయోనికి)The village public servant/messenger, traditionally drawn from marginalized communities, responsible for manual execution of feudal announcements.
Dappu dhaatidhu (దప్పుదాటిద్దురు)A localized expression meaning to beat the Dappu (leather drum) forcefully to sound a public alarm or proclaim a village decree.
Karaabu chaetthuru (కరాబుచేత్తురట)A raw regional adaptation of the Urdu word Kharaab, used in this context as a historical euphemism for sexual assault, abduction, and violation of bodily modesty.

Linguistic & Ethnographic Commentary

Cultural Interpretation

Swaroopa’s account gives a powerful ethnographic view of how gender solidarity and subaltern political history intersect within folk religion. The narrative reveals that Bathukamma’s origins are firmly rooted in a collective trauma response. The folk memory captures the deep structural terror faced by women under the Razakar and Nizam regimes, where subaltern female bodies were highly vulnerable to state-backed feudal abduction ("knaopadh-thae pattukopoyi karaabu chaetthuru"). The myth of the hidden girl transforming into flowers is a profound psychological mechanism of survival: it converts a tragic disappearance or violation into a divine, immortalized victory of nature over feudal predators. The phrase "Bathukamma" (Live, Mother!) thus functions as a literal, defiant political invocation for female life and bodily autonomy.

Variation & Change

The speaker highlights a massive structural decline in the festival's duration, contrasting the historical one-month celebration (Naela rojulu) with the modernized, state-sponsored nine-day format (Thommidi rojulu). This contraction reflects the intense shifts in women's labor dynamics, where modern agricultural schedules and wage-labor demands leave less room for prolonged, month-long communal ritual spaces.

Research Note

This text is a masterclass resource for research in subaltern feminist historiography and the political memory of Telangana. It documents how marginalized women used flower-centric religious spaces to archive historical trauma and resistance, providing crucial raw data for tracking the transition of folk-religious practices from feudal control into modern cultural performance [INDEX].
