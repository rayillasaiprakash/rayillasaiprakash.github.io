Holi / Kaamuni Panduga (Kaama Dahanam)

👤 Mahender🎂 Age 45💼 Police Constable (Formerly from a rural background)📍 Jagtial district, Telangana

Speaker Metadata

Mahender, age 45 · Police Constable (Formerly from a rural background) — Jagtial district, Telangana.

Original Telangana Narrative

కామునిపండుగా.. హోలీ అంటే కామునిపండుగు ఆప్పట్ల. కాముడాడుదురు, కామదానంజెయ్యాలంటే పిలగాళ్లందరు కొలలాడుదురు, ఆడోల్లు సప్పట్లుగొట్టుకుంట కాముడాడుదురు. ఇండ్లపోన్న తోరుగుదురు, ఇంత మక్కలో, బియ్యమో, పప్పో, పైసలో ఇద్దురు ఎవలన్న. అవ్వివట్టుకొని కోమట్లింటికోయ్యి అవ్విటికీ పైసలిత్తె పైసలు లేకపోతే ఎమన్న కొనుక్కతిందురు. ఇగ ఆకరినాడు ఆకోలలు కచ్చిర్కాడ కామదికాడ అనుంట్టుండే, ఇగ అందరు ఆడుకొని ఎవలుముందత్తే ఆల్లు కాముడు అంట్టువెట్టి ఆకోలలు అన్డ్లేద్దురు. అన్డ్లేసి కాలవెట్టి ఇంటికద్దురు. కామదానం అంటదే. ఇగ తెల్లారి రంగులువుసుకొని హోలీ ఆడుతరు. అది మొన్నమోన్నచ్చిందట మా తాతన్నడు.

English Translation

Kaamuni Panduga... back in those days, Holi was called Kaamuni Panduga. They would play Kaamudu. To perform the ritual burning of the God of Desire (Kaama Dahanam), young boys would play a rhythmic stick-dance (Kolalaadudhuru), while the women would dance and clap their hands (Sappatlu gottukunta). They would wander from house to house; people would give them some corn, rice, lentils, or coins. Taking these items, they would visit the merchant’s (Komati) shop; the merchant would either exchange those grains for cash or give them snacks to eat. On the final day, those dancing sticks were stacked at the village square (Kacchiri) where the Kaamudu pyre was built. After everyone finished dancing, whoever arrived first would ignite the Kaamudu pyre and toss those dancing sticks into the fire. After burning it completely, they would return home. That is what Kaama Dahanam means. The next morning, they would smear colors onto each other and play Holi. My grandfather used to say that this practice of playing with colors arrived only very recently.

Festival Practices

Rhythmic folk stick-dancing (Kolalu) by young boys; organized clapping circle-dances (Kaamudaadu) by village women; door-to-door resource pooling (collecting corn, rice, lentils, and small coins); traditional barter or transactional conversion of gathered grains at the merchant's (Komati) shop for festive sweets or cash; gathering at the communal village square (Kacchiri); constructing a public pyre for Kaama Dahanam; competitive torching of the pyre; burning the dancing sticks as part of the sacrificial flame; and playing with colors on the subsequent morning.

Festival Vocabulary

TermMeaning
Kaamuni Panduga / Kaamudaadu (కామునిపండుగా/కాముడాడుదురు)The authentic, localized non-Sanskritized name for the festival, centering the narrative entirely on Kamadeva (the God of Desire) and regional folklore rather than the North Indian Holika myth.
Kolalaadudhuru (కొలలాడుదురు)A rhythmic folk performance using wooden sticks (Kolalu) struck against each other, traditionally performed by village youths during the counting days leading up to the bonfire.
Komati (కోమట్లు)The local trading and grocery-vending caste, functioning as the foundational micro-bank and grain-exchange node in the village economy.
Kacchiri (కచ్చిర్కాడ)The historical village square, open-air community court, or the platform outside the village headman's house where public assemblies and high-status rituals occur.
Anttuwetti (అంట్టువెట్టి)A highly localized dialectal verb meaning to set on fire, ignite, or touch a flame to a pyre.
Monna Monnacchindhata (మొన్నమోన్నచ్చిందట)A distinct idiomatic expression literally meaning "it arrived only the day before yesterday," used here by the speaker to denote a very recent cultural import or a late historical modification of an ancient indigenous ritual.

Linguistic & Ethnographic Commentary

Cultural Interpretation

Mahender’s field narrative tracks how a festival’s core theological focus can be completely transformed on the ground through subaltern oral memory. Textual Sanskritized Hinduism maps Holi onto the Puranic myth of Prahlada and the burning of the demoness Holika. However, the rural Telangana memory completely bypasses this script. It frames the festival exclusively around Kaamuni Panduga—the destruction and subsequent rebirth of Kamadeva (desire/nature’s fertility).

Variation & Change

The 45-year-old speaker introduces an excellent piece of oral historiography by quoting his grandfather (Ma thaathannadu). The grandfather’s memory identifies the modern practice of throwing synthetic wet colors (Rangulu) as a highly superficial, very recent cultural import (monna monnacchindhi). Historically, the festival was strictly an ascetic and dramatic celebration centered around the bonfire, body disciplines, and rhythmic oral songs, rather than the commercialized, color-throwing spectacle seen today.

Research Note

This field narrative is a phenomenal resource for a doctoral project analyzing cultural assimilation, regional resistance, and the shift from pre-commercial folk rituals to popularized modern festivals. It explicitly documents how Telangana’s native Kaamuni Panduga was gradually overwritten by the commercialized, North Indian model of Holi, preserving valuable data on historical micro-economies and subaltern spatial navigation [INDEX].
