Sankuraathri / Katrevula Panduga (Makar Sankranti & Cattle Rituals)

👤 Sudhayya🎂 Age 53💼 Farmer📍 Jagtial district, Telangana

Speaker Metadata

Sudhayya, age 53 · Farmer — Jagtial district, Telangana.

Original Telangana Narrative

సంకురాత్రి అచ్చిందంటే మన సంపర్దాయం పకారం పిండికురాలు అండుకోవాలే, తీపిజేస్కోవలె అంటే, ఇక్కడ గుద్దవలగా కోస్కతిన్దురు. కోన్ని కోస్కుందురు అందరు , అందరుగవ్వే పులుసో – బుట్టనో నాకుదురు. పొద్దుగాల లేషి ఇల్లలికి కడుపలకు, అరుగులకు, మొగురాలకు జాజువూసి. గిరుకతోని గీతల ముగ్గులు ఇల్లంతేత్తురు. ఇంటిముంగట ముగ్గేసి అండ్ల గొబ్బెమ్మలువెట్టి ధాన్యాలు, నేరగాయలు ఎత్తురు. ఆంధ్రకేయ్యేమో మంచిపండుగా అని మంచిగ మొక్కుతే, ఈళ్ళు పీడపండుగు ఆ పండుగీపండుగని ఈల్లేమో దానిమీదికేల్లి వోవాలె దీనిమీదికేల్లి వోవాలని ఒకనాల్గు రోజులముందు కాట్రేవుల పండుగ అని చేద్దురు. కాట్రేవుల పండుగ అంటే, ఎనుకట ఊల్లె ఎడ్లు, గొడ్లు, బర్ర్లఅన్ని ఒక్కమోకేయ్యి కొట్టుకపోయ్యి కాసుకద్దురు ఎనుకట. కాటిదేవునికి ఏందంటే, ఒక్కపోద్దుండి బెల్లబువ్వండి కల్లు అవన్నీ మిరుపకాయ దొండకాయ ఆన్ని ఏకూరగాయలుంటే ఆకురగాయలు దండలుగుచ్చి మావిడి కొమ్మలుగుచ్చి ఓకాడ దోరణంగట్టి అక్కడికిక్కడికి ఆయింత ఈ ఒక్కపొద్దున్నొడు అవిట్టెంబడి, ఆ బర్లుగొడ్ల ఎంబడి సుట్టు ఉరుకుతే ఓలొట్టి, ఉడుకుడుకు బువ్వ, ముద్దలుతోని, ఆఒక్కపొద్దున్నొడు ఆ ఎడ్లగొడ్లసుట్టు ఉరుకంగ ఈ ఉడుకుముద్దలు వట్టుకొని, బరివాతనే ఉండాలే ఈ ఒక్కపొద్దున్నోడు. వాడు ఉర్రుకుతే ఆ ఉడుకుడుకుముద్దలు దొప్పల వట్టుకొని ఈపుల సంపుతే వావ్వ - వాయ్యా అనుకుంట ఉరుకు. అది కాట్రేవుల పండుగ అందురు. ఎగవడి ఉరికి ఒకోమ్ముకు సంపినవలె ఇగ ఎగవడి మంద ఇడిశినవలే ఇగ ఆవిటెంబడివడి ఊల్లెకిఅత్తుము. ఊల్లె సోచ్చినంక అవ్విటికీ జాజో కలర్లో అవ్విటిపెయ్యికి కొట్టుదుము.

English Translation

When Sankuraathri arrives, according to our tradition (Sampardaayam), we must cook fried/sweet flour savories (Pindikuraalu). If we want to make it sweet, here people slaughter and eat a sheep or goat (Guddavalaga kosi thindhuru). Everyone slaughters a fowl and tastes the meat curry (Pulso) or the seasoned rice (Butthano). Waking up early in the morning, we mop the house (Illaliki), smearing red mud (Jaaju) onto the doorsteps, verandas, and the bases of the pillars (Moguraalu). Using a rolling stencil tool (Giruka), we decorate the entire house with geometric chalk lines. In front of the house, we draw a large design, place Gobbemmalu (cow-dung balls) in it, and scatter grains and seasonal wild berries (Naeragaayalu). While the coastal Andhra people treat this as a highly auspicious festival and worship devoutly, the local people here look at it as a time of shifting away misfortune (Peeda Panduga). To ward off evils and clear the paths, they celebrate Kaatrevula Panduga about four days prior. In the old days, for Kaatrevula Panduga, they would gather all the village oxen, sheep, and buffaloes into a single massive herd and drive them to the forest clearing. For the deity of the wilderness (Kaati Devudu), the one observing the fast cooks sweet jaggery-rice (Bellambuwwa) and offers toddy (Kallu). They string together whatever vegetables are available—chillies, ivy gourds—along with mango leaves, and tie them as a festive arch (Dhoranam) across the path. The person observing the fast must run stark naked (Barivaathana) along with the cattle, encircling the entire herd. While he runs around the oxen and buffaloes, others carry small leaf-bowls (Doppala) filled with steaming hot rice balls (Uduku muddhalu) and forcefully pelt them onto his bare back. As they strike his back, he runs crying out in pain, "Wavva! Vayya!" That is what they call Kaatrevula Panduga. Running forward with great force, once the hot rice strikes the cattle's horns and the herd is unleashed, we chase them back into the village. Once they enter the village, we paint their bodies with red mud (Jaaju) or vibrant colors.

Festival Practices

Preparing traditional fried pastries (Pindikuraalu); ritual slaughter of livestock (Guddavalaga) and poultry; cleaning and smearing the house with cow-dung; painting doorsteps and architectural pillars with red earth (Jaaju); applying geometric chalk patterns using a mechanical roller (Giruka); placing cow-dung mounds (Gobbemmalu) with grains and wild berries (Naeragaayalu) at the threshold; observing Kaatrevula Panduga (worship of the forest protector deity Kaati Devudu) to ward off village misfortunes (Peeda); gathering the entire settlement's livestock into a singular collective herd; preparing ritual jaggery-rice (Bellambuwwa) and fermenting liquor (Kallu) offerings; constructing a defensive vegetative archway (Dhoranam) using threaded vegetables (chillies, ivy gourds) and mango leaves; a male ritual actor running completely naked (Barivaathana) around the running herd; ritual pelting of boiling-hot rice balls (Uduku muddhalu) from leaf-bowls onto the bare back of the naked runner; unleashing the stampeding herd back into the settlement; and painting the hides of the cattle with Jaaju or synthetic colors upon re-entry.

Festival Vocabulary

TermMeaning
Pindikuraalu (పిండికురాలు)Regional terms for deep-fried or steamed flour-based festive snacks and savories.
Guddavalaga (గుద్దవలగా)A highly specific dialectal expression denoting a sheep or goat selected for slaughter.
Butthano (బుట్టనో)A local term for seasoned, compressed festive rice or a specific preparation of meat-infused rice.
Moguraalu (మొగురాలకు)The structural wooden or stone pillars that support the roof of traditional rural Telangana houses.
Jaaju (జాజు)A natural, iron-rich red oxide earth used to paint borders on thresholds and walls during protective rituals.
Giruka (గిరుక)A specialized, rolling hollow tube tool with punctured patterns used to rapidly roll out complex geometric powder designs on the floor.
Peeda Panduga (పీడపండుగు)"The Festival of Warding Off Afflictions/Evils," showing a stark theological difference from typical textbook definitions of Sankranti.
Kaati Devudu / Kaatrevu (కాటిదేవునికి)The fierce guardian deity of the village boundaries, wilderness, and pastures, responsible for shielding livestock from epidemics and wildlife predators.
Barivaathana (బరివాతనే)Completely naked / devoid of any apparel.
Doppala (దొప్పల)Leaf-bowls stitched together from dry Banyan or Palasa leaves, used to hold hot ritual food.

Linguistic & Ethnographic Commentary

Cultural Interpretation

Sudhayya’s narrative provides brilliant ethnographic data on how agrarian communities conceptualize boundary maintenance, animal health, and regional identity. His direct contrast between Coastal Andhra practice ("Andhrastole... manchi panduga") and Telangana practice ("Peeda Panduga") shows how the subaltern communities of Telangana view Sankranti. Instead of framing it purely as a joyous solar harvest festival, the local memory views it as a critical, high-risk transition period where malignant spiritual entities and diseases (Peeda) must be physically and ritually driven away from the settlement's primary capital: its livestock.

Variation & Change

The speaker repeatedly inserts the marker Enukata (in the old days) to describe the collective driving of the entire village's cattle into a singular herd. This highlights a shift from historical communal pasture management and shared ritual labor to a highly individualized, fragmented contemporary setup where these complex, high-discipline public body rituals have mostly disappeared.

Research Note

This text is incredibly useful for dissertations dealing with South Asian subaltern performance, boundary rituals, and veterinary folklore. It preserves rare evidence of ritualized human flagellation using food elements (Uduku muddhalu) to secure herd immunity. It provides empirical verification of how pre-modern peasant communities weaponized pain, nudity, and raw landscape markers to protect their cattle from environmental and supernatural hazards [INDEX].
