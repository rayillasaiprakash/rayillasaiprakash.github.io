Dasara (Vijayadashami)

👤 Narsayya🎂 Age 84💼 Farmer📍 Jagtial district, Telangana

Speaker Metadata

Narsayya, age 84 · Farmer — Jagtial district, Telangana.

Original Telangana Narrative

మక్కలు ఎంచి సత్హు చేస్కుందురు బియ్యం ఎంచి సత్హు చేస్కుందురు తెల్లజోన్న్లు ఎంచి సత్తు చేస్కుందురు. ఒక్క దసరకు కొన్న చీరే నాలుగైదు దసరలకి కట్టుకున్డుము పైసల్లెక. బిన్ను జాకిట్లువట్టిచ్చుకుంటే పెద్ద, నేశిన కనుములేమో చిన్న. వంటలేమో ఉన్నోల్లేమో ఉన్నతీరు లేనోల్లేమో లేనోల్లెతీరు చేస్కతింద్దురు. జొన్నలు, మక్కలు పదనజేసి బెట్ట్రిచ్చి ఎండవోసి మల్ల నానవోసి వడవోసి దంచి సాకినాలు గారప్పలు చేస్కుందుము. రెండుమూడోద్దుల ముందాలనే బెట్లిచ్చి, ఎండవోత్తుము. ఎండవోసి మల్ల పోటువేట్టుడుము. తెల్లగా బియ్యమోలె పోటువెట్టి దంచుదుము దంచి చేసుకుందుము. ఇగ పండుగ నాడు కోడినో మేకనో కోసుకొని ఇన్ని గుడాలు పోసుకొని తిన్దుము. పైసలున్నోడు మేకనుకోసుకును పైసల్లెనోడు కోడినో – బుట్టనో కోసుకొని ఒడ్దేకిచ్చుకును. ఇగ జంబి, కొన్నక్కల్ల పొద్దుగాలనే కొడుదురు. పొద్దుగాలనే జంబి పిల్లలచ్చు అప్పుడు దొరలున్నపుడు ఎట్టి పిల్లలచ్చు. సాకలోడు కొట్టేటోడు, తేనుగోడు బోనం అన్దేటోడు. దేవునికాడికిపొయి సారుగమ్మ కాడికివొయ్యి పూదిచ్చి, పోగేసి, పిల్లకు బొట్టువెట్టి జెడ్తిచ్చినంక ఆయింత అటోగలు ఇటోగలువట్టి సోప్పకట్ట వెట్టి పిల్లనిగొట్టి జమ్బాకు తెంపుకోని అత్తురు. ఇంటికచ్చి జమ్మి ఆకు ఇంట్ల పెద్దోళ్లకి చేతులవెట్టి కాళ్ళు మొక్కీ దీవెనలు తీసుకొందురు. ఎవడన్న పగున్నోడు అక్కన్నే లోల్లివేట్టుకొన్ని కొట్టుకుందురు.

English Translation

We would select corn, rice, or white sorghum to prepare the roasted flour mixture (Satthu). Due to the lack of money, a saree bought for one Dasara would be worn for four or five subsequent Dasara festivals. If we got blouses tailored, they would be oversized, but the woven borders (Kanumulu) were always too small. As for the festive cooking, the rich ate according to their wealth, and the poor managed with whatever little they had. We would soak sorghum and corn until they reached the right moisture level (Padana), let them sprout slightly (Betthricchi), dry them in the sun, then soak them again, drain them, and pound them to make Sakinalu and Garappalu. Two or three days in advance, we would let the grains sprout and dry them. After drying, we would pound them into a fine flour (Potu). We would pound them until the flour looked as white as polished rice, and then prepare the snacks. On the festival day, we would slaughter a fowl or a goat and eat it along with boiled spiced legumes (Gudaalu). Those with money would slaughter a goat, while those without money would slaughter a fowl or a small bird and manage to pull through (Oddekicchukunu). The ritual cutting of the Jambi (Shami tree branches) used to happen early in the morning. Back then, during the era of the feudal landlords (Doralu), the Jambi branches were brought down through forced caste labor (Etti). The washerman (Saakalodu) was the one who chopped it, and the toddy-tapper (Thaenugodu) was responsible for handling the food offerings (Bonam). They would go to the deity, visit the village border goddess (Saarugamma), offer incense, build a ritual mound, apply a tilak to the branch, and after performing the sacrifice (Jedthicchinanka), men from both sides would hold dried millet stalks (Soppakatta), strike the branch, pluck the Jambi leaves, and return home. They give the jambi leaves to the elders in their home and take blessings. If there was any pre-existing enmity between groups, they would start a massive brawl and fight right there at the ritual site.

Festival Practices

Hand-selecting local grains (corn, rice, white sorghum) to prepare ritual Satthu flour; multi-day processing of grain involving moisture balancing (Padana), sprouting (Betthricchi), sun-drying, and pounding (Potu) to bake Sakinalu and Garappalu; animal or poultry sacrifice based on household economic stratification; preparation of Gudaalu (boiled legumes); early morning Jambi (Jammichettu/Shami tree) worship driven by Etti (feudal forced labor); hierarchical division of ritual duties based on sub-castes (Washerman/Chakali chops the wood, Tenugu/Toddy-tapper handles the Bonam); worshiping the village border deity (Saarugamma); ritual flagellation of the sacred branch using millet stalks (Soppakatta); collective plucking of Shami leaves ("gold"); and venting hidden structural factionalism or interpersonal enmities through physical brawls at the boundary site.

Festival Vocabulary

TermMeaning
Satthu (సత్హు/సత్తు)A traditional energy-dense festive powder made of roasted, pounded grains mixed with jaggery.
Kanumulu (కనుములేమో)The distinct woven borders or selvages of handloom fabrics and traditional sarees.
Padana (పదనజేసి)The precise level of optimal moisture or dampness required in grains before processing.
Betthricchi (బెట్ట్రిచ్చి/బెట్లిచ్చి)A distinct regional agricultural term meaning to allow soaked grains to split or slightly germinate under controlled conditions.
Potu (పోటువేట్టుడుము)The heavy, rhythmic pounding of grains using a massive wooden pestle (Onka) in a stone mortar to achieve ultra-fine flour.
Oddekicchukunu (ఒడ్దేకిచ్చుకును)A deep Telangana idiomatic expression meaning to somehow stay afloat, survive a crisis, or successfully manage a hard situation despite minimal resources.
Jambi (జంబి)The Shami tree (Prosopis cineraria), central to Vijayadashami folklore as a symbol of victory, prosperity, and structural rejuvenation.
Etti (ఎట్టి)A brutal historical system of unpaid, caste-enforced feudal labor extracted by Doralu (landlords) in Telangana.
Jedthicchinanka (జెడ్తిచ్చినంక)A dialectal variant meaning to deliver a strike, finish a sacrifice, or complete a ritual severing.
Soppakatta (సోప్పకట్ట)A tied bundle of dry sorghum or millet stalks used as fodder or, in this context, as a ritual hitting tool.

Linguistic & Ethnographic Commentary

Cultural Interpretation

Narsayya’s narrative offers a raw look at how class boundaries and feudal politics heavily shaped the celebration of Dasara in historical Telangana. His memories strip away the romanticized view of rural harmony by directly mapping economic disparity onto the festive kitchen ("unnoallemo unnathiru, laenoallemo laenoallu"). For poor peasants, celebrating meant stretching a single festive garment across five years and using semantic resilience (Oddekicchukunu) to survive the financial strain of the holiday.

Variation & Change

The 84-year-old speaker paints a clear picture of change by contrasting the historical Dora era of forced labor (Etti) with contemporary times. The multi-day, labor-intensive preparation of Sakinalu flour through physical pounding (Potu) captures a pre-mechanized lifestyle where festive food required intense, collective bodily labor.

Research Note

This field note is a stellar resource for subaltern studies, Dalit-Bahujan history, and the political economy of festivals in South Asia. It clearly documents how a festival celebrated as a "universal victory of good over evil" was experienced on the ground as a site of caste-enforced labor extraction, resource scarcity, and socio-political friction [INDEX].
